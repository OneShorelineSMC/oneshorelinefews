    How the three gauges compare

    The difference between two gauges’ highest water in the same month. Monthly highs are used rather than daily ones because the same high tide can fall either side of midnight at two gauges. “In the highest months”: the top 10% of months at the second gauge, to see whether the gap holds when the Bay is high (a bigger gap means the difference grows in storms and King Tides). Useful for judging how well Alameda or San Francisco stand in for a place nearer Redwood City.

    Highest days on record

    The 15 highest days (one per storm or tide event: days within 3 days of a higher one are left out), with the other gauges’ highest over the same day and the days either side.

    How this works
    • Data: NOAA’s high and low water record at each gauge, in ft NAVD88, downloaded a few years each hour by the tide tracker’s data job (scripts/tide_tracker.py, kept in tide_tracker_history.json on the data branch). Each day’s value is the highest high water. The current water year is on the Tide tracker page, not here.
    • Years: water years (Oct 1–Sep 30), named by the year they end. Yearly numbers use only years with at least 80% of their days.
    • Trend: a straight line fitted to each year’s value (least squares), in ft per decade. The average daily high moves with sea level; the highest water each year also moves with storms and El Niño, so its trend is noisier.
    • NAVD88 over the decades: NOAA gives old data in NAVD88 using today’s datum relation, so a real rise in sea level shows up as a rise; a gauge that was moved or re-leveled can show a step.
